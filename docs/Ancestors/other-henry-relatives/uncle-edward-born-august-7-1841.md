### United States Express Guard to Government Money

**Edward Everett Henry, Age 64**  
(Born August 7, 1841)

* **Hours of Firing at Practice:** 10 A.M. to 12 P.M.
* **Trips to United States Treasury:** Washington, D.C.
* **To United States Mint:** Philadelphia, PA

**Memorandums:**

* Generally three wrecks a year.
* No sleeping or leaving of money safes or boxes until delivery is made.
* Sub-Treasury for the Western States: Chicago.

**Gold Bullion:**

* From Denver and Pueblo Smelters to U.S. Mint Philadelphia, Month of June 1903, nearly seven million dollars in gold.

**Arms:**

* Colt's Automatic 7-Shooter, Maxim Attachment.
* Winchester, Magazine, 18 shots in all.

**Additional Items:**

* Basket with rations and heavy overcoat.

### Summary

Uncle Edward, offended by Father’s criticism of his irresponsible behavior, retaliated by anonymously sending false accusations to high-ranking Republican officials in 1875, urging Father’s dismissal from government service. The plot was quickly uncovered, Father was fully exonerated with help from General James A. Garfield, and the matter was officially closed. Although the incident caused lasting family estrangement, the narrative defends Father’s character, portraying him as stern but fair, deeply generous, widely respected, and admired for both his integrity and kindness.

### Below is a modern rewrite of pages 257 and 258 of the Book "Captain Henry of Geauga"

In 1871, after marrying the kind and patient Annie Langworthy, Uncle Edward often earned deserved criticism from Father for his irresponsible behavior. Most of these antics were meant as jokes, but they were not harmless pranks. Still, much of the family forgave these lapses because of his bravery in battle and his later devotion to his wife, whose fragile health eventually left her completely disabled.

In the summer of 1875, Father—perhaps speaking too harshly—scolded Edward for persuading Grandmother to give him a large share of the income from her small dairy. Father insisted that Edward return to work instead of living off her generosity, which ultimately meant living at Father’s expense. Edward took deep offense. In retaliation, he later wrote two anonymous letters under the fake name “Duncan Ely, Chairman of the Republican Committee, Blissells, Ohio,” claiming they came from the “Nineteenth District Republican Rooms” in Ohio.

One letter was sent to Marshall Jewell, the postmaster general, and the other to Senator John Sherman of Ohio. Both accused Father of abusing his office and urged that he be fired from government service, listing detailed but false charges and claiming that Father endangered local Republican success. Fortunately, Father already knew Governor Jewell fairly well, having met him in Washington and traveled with him by train earlier that summer.

Father barely knew Senator Sherman, who forwarded the letter to the postmaster general. Though alarmed, Jewell sent both letters to General James A. Garfield, asking him to address the issue directly with Father. As soon as this happened, the true author of the letters was discovered and the accusations were exposed as lies.

Father quickly obtained letters clearing his name from Dr. H. W. Curtiss of Chagrin Falls and A. G. Kent of Bainbridge, both respected Republicans in the area. These letters defended Father without revealing who had written the forged accusations. General Garfield sent them to Governor Jewell along with a strong endorsement of Father. In a reply dated October 15, 1875, the postmaster general officially closed the matter, stating that he had never doubted Father’s honesty, ability, or good character, and that no further action was needed.

A later letter suggests that Mother also intervened, writing a spirited and effective defense of Father to the postmaster general.

In a personal letter, General Garfield congratulated Father on the successful outcome, expressed confidence that the department would see the truth, and reassured him not to worry about Jewell’s reaction. Garfield also commented on politics, his legal work, and sent warm regards to the family. In a postscript, he humorously noted seeing Edward at a teachers’ meeting, smiling as though he had never delivered a forged letter to the federal government.

Edward and his wife later died childless. With no risk of causing harm, the narrator feels justified in recounting these events—not out of spite, but to explain official records that might otherwise unfairly reflect on Father, and to defend him from blame for the lasting family estrangement that followed.

Father’s job sometimes required him to use a stern tone and intimidating presence, aided by his piercing eyes and deep voice. He likely did not realize how overwhelming this could be. People accused by him often felt compelled to confess, and some even said he could make an innocent person feel guilty. As a result, some people felt hurt by what seemed like excessive severity.

Yet while this sternness earned him a few enemies, it won him many more friends. Father was generous and kind, helping countless young men secure government jobs and assisting people in financial trouble. He disliked dishonesty and ingratitude, but he was deeply loyal to his friends and widely admired for his warmth and sincerity. Even convicted criminals sometimes sought his advice and help after serving their sentences. A former newspaper editor, James B. Morrow, praised Father publicly, writing that “his intellect is keen and his heart is kind.”

Finally, the narrator explains that much of this account relies on correspondence between Father and General Garfield from the decade before Garfield became president in 1880. Although quoting these letters at length may seem excessive, they reflect a genuine friendship that Father’s descendants can rightly take pride in.
