Jim Henry and his wife, Grace, have spent a significant amount of time researching the life of Jim’s famous great-great grandfather, the late Civil War Captain, Charles Henry.

The Henrys, who now live in Bainbridge near what locals refer to as the “Big House on the hill” that once belonging to Capt. Henry himself, recently gave a presentation about his family and their local roots to the Bainbridge Historical Society.

Using the 1942 book “Captain Henry of Geauga” written by Henry’s son, Fredrick, the Henrys presented a fascinating portrait about one of Bainbridge Township’s favorite sons.

The event was attended by many Henry family members as well as Mike and Amanda Reiser, current owners of the Henry house, which overlooks Geauga Lake Road where several decendants of the Henry family have built their homes in more recent years.

Also at the event were two descendants of Nils Jorgenson, who was a tenant at one of Capt. Henry’s farms.

In addition to his time serving in the Civil War, Capt. Henry was a well-known friend and associate of James A. Garfield, who served in the U.S. House of Representatives prior to being elected President. Garfield, of course, was assassinated in 1881.

The story begins

Capt. Henry’s story starts with his grandparents, Simon and Rhoda Henry, who lived in New England. Together, they had 10 children including John, Capt. Henry’s father, who was born in 1835.

Jim Henry noted his own family’s direct descendent was John, the third oldest of the 10 siblings, who served in the War of 1812 along with his two older brothers.

While in New England, Simon and Rhoda Henry decided they wanted to move west for more land for their sons so Simon traded a 200-acre parcel in New England for 549 acres in Bainbridge Township in Geauga County.

His sons, John and Orrin, traveled to Bainbridge in 1816. Simon and Rhoda came the next year with eight children traveling 600 miles in 45 days, settling in Bainbridge in 1817.

The two men built a family cabin close to Chillicothe Road and after dividing the land, built another cabin near the Aurora branch of the Chagrin River, which crosses under what is now Pettibone Road. Simon Henry kept 100 acres for himself, while sons Orrin, William and John each took ownership of 100-acre parcels along Pettibone Road and near the river.

John Henry and his wife, Polly, built a cabin and he worked as a teacher and surveyor and had other odd jobs. Capt. Henry, was the seventh of their nine children.

Simon Henry, oldest son of John, built his first house on the hill on Geauga Lake Road and the house would later be known as the “Big House on the hill.” It was added on to a number of times over the years. John Henry and his family would eventually move to the house, where Charles Henry would grow up.

Capt. Henry quit school when he was 16 and eventually found his way to Michigan to work in a wood mill. Eventually, he and his brother-in-law went into a business building cheese boxes at Bridge Creek in Auburn Township. During that time, they were hearing about James Garfield and his influential work and speaking engagements. At the time, Mr. Garfield, who was born in nearby Moreland Hills, was the head teacher at the Western Reserve Eclectic Insitute, later known as Hiram College.

At the age of 22, Capt. Henry enrolled at the college and met Mr. Garfield. The two became good friends. While he was attending college, Charles sometimes took time out to work, including a job teaching at the Bridge Creek School in Auburn.

While a student, he met, Sophie, who would later become his wife. After graduation, he accepted a teaching job in Solon and after a younger brother enlisted in the Civil War, Capt. Henry helped magage the family farm in Bainbridge

It was at that time, that Mr. Garfield visited the Henry home and told him they needed to start a regiment for the Civil War.

Feeling inspired, he returned to Hiram College and was the first person to sign up to serve in the 42nd Regiment of the Union Army first as a sergeant and eventually a second and first lieutenant.

The soldiers thought so highly of him that during the Battle of Vicksburg, they began referring to him as Capt. Henry and the name stuck. Even Mr. Garfield would evenutlaly call him captain.

Wartime service

The regiment’s first battle was at Middle Creek in Eastern Kentucky and with then-Col. Garfield leading the charge, it was the Army’s first victory under a Garfield command. But his tenure was shortlived after he was tapped to lead more soldiers working directly with Gen. Ulysses S. Grant.

While Capt. Henry was fighting in the Battle of Vicksburg in Mississippi, he was shot in the foot and returned to Bainbridge on a furlough. He would later finish his three-year term in the war while serving in Louisiana.

After another brief trip back home to marry Sophie, he returned to Louisiana once again to work while sending money back to Ohio to buy tracts of land for him in Bainbridge.

Through an auction, he managed to buy a 106-acre tract of land known as the McClintock farm. With Sophie in tow, he spent another four or five years in Lousiana before returning to Bainbridge permanently to farmhis land and work as a postmaster at nearby Pond Station, a railroad stop next to Geauga Lake.

The work was hard on his health, and he later reached out to Mr. Garfield, who was then a congressman and living in Hiram.

With Congressman Garfield’s support, Capt. Henry was appointed to be a postal agent based in Cleveland. In that capacity, he traveled on the railroad as an inspector looking for people who were stealing from the post office.

During this same time, Congressman Garfield won the Republican nomination for President of the United States and was elected our 20th president in 1881.

As President, Garfield nominated Capt. Henry to serve as U.S. Marshal and remained in that role until a year after Gafield was assassinated, when he returned to Bainbridge once again.

Back home, he purchased another 165 acres called the Russ place on the north side of Pettibone Road. There, he built a sugar house and produced maple syrup. He would later buy another 55 acres of Willow Farm off Brewster Road.

By then, he owned some 400 acres encompassing the Russ land, Willow Farm, and the original home place.

In his capacity as an agent for the rail lines, Capt. Henry made his way to Texas for a bit, but his heart remained in Bainbridge and in his older years, he and Sophie returned home to celebrate the reunion of the 42nd Regiment. While there, his goal was to keep up the farm properties, put his children through college, and build resources to leave to his wife when he died.

But as was often the case for Capt. Henry, duty called. As the story goes, two of President Garfield’s sons reached out with news that an employee had taken off from a lumber company with $20,000, in tow and fled to Brazil.

The Garfield boys asked the good captain to help the bond company representing the lumber company find the employee and their money. In 1892, on Christmas Eve, Capt. Henry not only caught the thief, but also captured another thief from the same area who had embezzled another $40,000.

Coming home once again to Bainbridge, Capt. Henry had built such a name for himself capturing the fugitives, he was hired in a part-time capacity by another bond company doing the same type of work.

A hero laid to rest

Capt. Charles Henry died in 1906 and was buried in Evergreen Cemetery in Chagrin Falls. His wife Sophia spent her last six years at the “Big House on the hill.”

Today, that house remains the focus of attention for the Henry family. Six separate households with Henry descendants are located nearby.

“We are thrilled they came back,” said Mr. Henry, who noted that the return of his ancestor’s family to their original homestead will surely keep the Henry legacy alive for years to come.
