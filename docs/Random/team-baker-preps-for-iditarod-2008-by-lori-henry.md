MUSHERS My family will have six teams in this year’s Iditarod Sled Dog Race which starts on March 1st. My brother, John Baker, (shown in photo with me)will have two more teams driven by Tollef Monson (drove a team last year for John), and Benoit Gerard (rookie musher from France). Other family members also in the race include my uncle, Louis Nelson, (shown here with his wife, Lulu) and his two sons, Robert and Darin. My cousins, Robert and Darin (see photos), are both rookies in this year’s race.

MUSHER MEALS There is so much preparation for this race as you can imagine. I am responsible to cook ‘human’ meals for my brother’s kennel (3 mushers). And this year we added my cousins for a total of 5 mushers! I don’t do all the cooking by myself (thank goodness) and try to organize the many family chefs. We do a variety of breakfast, lunch, and dinner meals then baked goods for snacks and desserts. Each is pre-cooked, frozen, and vacuum sealed and ready to be warmed up by the mushers while they are on the trail. The meals must help keep them nourished yet look and taste appealing to each musher – this is where careful planning is key. This year we prepared a huge amount of food . . . 1,010 individual packets of meals . . . absolutely amazing!

While I was in France this year for school, I sampled different foods that I thought would be tasty and appealing after being frozen and warmed up. Thinking of Benoit, we made Coq au vin, Chicken Chasseur, Boeuf Bourguignon, and Cassoulet. All of these are cooked with wine or brandy so this will be a first to have such exotic, gourmet meals. We also packed up croissant sandwiches with sausage and cheese. After sampling everything, Benoit is not the only one who will be enjoying the French cuisine.

This year the Iditarod meal prep took 10 days of my time (as compared to 5 days last year) and I’ve traveled to Kotzebue twice for intense cooking marathons with my family.

DOG FOOD

My role in the cooking is just a small part, though, as there is so much more to do including chipping and sawing meat and fish, and bagging dry food for the dogs. Since the dogs need about 12,000 calories per day that means the mushers need to send out about 1,500# of dog food per team. Patrick has gone to Kotzebue once (and may do another trip) to help chip and cut the fish and meat.

Dog Booties

Then, there are the dog booties . . . with 16 dogs x 4 = 64 booties x 19 checkpoints – 1,216 booties (about one booty per mile). After sewn, they each need to be turned and bundled together so a musher can easily and efficiently booty each dog. And since some dogs paws are size ‘medium’ and some are size ‘large’ there is the sorting based on which dogs are in each team. Here in this photo, John’s wife, Iva, who  thought she was done with all of the booties but was just told that her husband may switch two of the dogs so the sizes may be different than what she has prepared for. Iva did the booties for 3 teams for a total of 3,648 booties!
