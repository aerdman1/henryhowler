## About Dad's Last Say

In this video, Dad (Gene) talks about me (Dan) visiting him for 20 days. At that time, Cindy and I were living in New Stuyahok, Alaska; a small Yupik community on the Nushagak River in Southeast Alaska near Dillingham.
Chip called me and said, "Dad's on the way out, you need to come home now." I made arrangements and got on the next flight I could, thinking I might only have days. The ticket was open-ended, knowing that Dad could go at any time.
When I got home to Ohio, Dad seemed to rally. He was so improved that he, Mom, and I took a trip south to Kidron, Ohio. Kidron is the home of Lehman's Amish Hardware. Dad wanted to see Lehman's. Mom made arrangements for us to stay in an interesting inn in the area.

In the video, you see Dad being very itchy. While we were staying at the inn, Dad wanted to take a bath, the one thing that helped with the itchiness. He needed help getting out of the tub. Seeing his body in the tub, I was shocked at how badly his body had been ravaged by the cancer.

When we got home, Dad and I would sit together for long periods of time, reading our books and not needing to talk a lot. When I got antsy for something to do, I would ask Dad what things would make him more comfortable. Dad mentioned that his favorite wooden rocking chair wasn’t comfortable anymore because he had lost most of the padding on his butt. I used Linda’s sewing machine and made a custom cushion for that chair. After that, Mom asked for a handrail for the stairs going up to their bedroom.

One day I remember asking Dad, “Is there anything you regard as unfinished business that I could help you resolve?” He thought for a minute and then admitted that there was. He got quiet and seemed to be preparing to share something with me that was significant. And then he just came out with it.
Dad said, “When I was cleaning my muzzleloader rifle, I got the cleaning tip stuck in the bottom of the barrel. I'd love to have that taken care of before I go.”

So he and I got the gun out, tried a few things, scratched our heads… and then Dad suggested, “Maybe we could shoot it out.” At this point the barrel was off the gun. We put the barrel in a vice, removed the nipple that held the firing caps, and sprinkled as much gunpowder as we could directly into the nipple hole. When no more powder would go in, we replaced the nipple, and put a firing cap on the nipple. There was an old sleeping bag in the garage, destined for the dump. We used it as a catcher’s mitt for the cleaning tip projectile.

Normally, when you pull the trigger on a muzzleloader, a small hammer contacts the firing cap, igniting the powder in the barrel and causing the gun to fire. We didn’t know exactly how much powder we were able to get in the barrel. And we didn’t know to what degree the cleaning tip was stuck in the barrel. There was a small chance of the barrel exploding when the cap was fired.

With Mom and Dad watching from a safe distance, I was all ready with a real hammer to strike the cap. Just as I raised the hammer, Dad said, “Wait, let me do that. You have more to live for.” It seemed reasonable, so he and I switched places. When he struck the cap, a significant flame burst from the barrel of the gun, expelling the cleaning tip and at the same time, catching the sleeping bag on fire. After putting out the small fire, we considered the entire affair a huge success.

At some point, communications from Cindy let me know that my return to Alaska was needed by both the school, her, and the boys. I talked to Dad about it. He insisted I return and that he didn’t want me hanging around waiting for him to die. On the day I was leaving, I remember looking at him and saying, “I don’t think I’ll see you again and I’m not sure what to say except… I love you and will miss you terribly.” He responded, “I love you too.” We all knew we were loved. And Dad made us feel loved, but we rarely heard it said.

It just so happened that as I was leaving, Pat was coming home. While I was with Dad, he was relatively alert, mobile, talkative (for Dad) and in good spirits. I have always thought of that time with Dad as a real gift. Unfortunately for Pat and Dad, Pat had an opposite experience. While Pat was with Dad, he declined markedly: increased nausea, sleeping most of the day, restlessness in sleep, and general discomfort.

My time with Dad made me realize that I needed Cindy, David, and Peter to know and never doubt how much I loved and cherished them. And that goes for most of the rest of you hoseheads too.
