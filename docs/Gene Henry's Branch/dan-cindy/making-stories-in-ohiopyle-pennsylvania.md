Peter was 9, and David was 12. We were with a large family group preparing to raft the Youghiogheny River—known to locals as “the Yawk”—in Ohiopyle State Park, about 65 miles southeast of Pittsburgh.David had his own inflatable kayak—a “ducky”—and quickly proved himself a confident, capable paddler. Peter, on the other hand, was disappointed to learn he couldn’t paddle solo. Instead, he’d have to be in a raft or tandem boat. So, he and I teamed up in a double ducky.
When rafting the Youghiogheny, you’ve got two main options: the Upper River, mostly flat with gentle Class I and II rapids, or the Lower River, which features bigger, faster Class III and IV rapids—thrilling, but potentially dangerous, especially at high water. Our group was tackling the Lower.
With the park rangers’ green light, we pushed off. David paddled on his own, always in sight, clearly in his element.
At each major rapid, guides stationed on the banks shouted instructions to help us navigate. At first, Peter wasn’t thrilled to be stuck with me—but that changed once he discovered his purpose on the river.
After each rapid, we’d often see empty boats drifting solo—evidence that someone had dumped. That’s when Peter and I kicked into action.
We’d grab the paddle, then pull alongside the loose kayak. Peter would leap in, steer it back to its paddler—usually waiting on the shore—and hop back into our boat. We felt like river lifeguards. Eventually, we started hanging back on purpose, going last through the rapids to spot stragglers and lend a hand when needed.
Once, Kim—slightly older than Peter—was tossed from her raft and swept into a particularly large rapid. At first, she looked terrified. But then her expression changed—she lit up, grinning and waving like she was on a roller coaster. She crested a wave and landed gently on the edge of a large rock mid-rapid. She crawled to the high point of the rock.
There she was, stranded on a boulder in the middle of one of the biggest rapids—still smiling and waving as other boaters floated by.

Peter and I knew we had to time it just right. As we moved into position, we called for Kim to jump—and she did, landing in our boat mid-rapid. We ferried her back to her raft through the swirling water, all three of us laughing the whole way.By the end of the day, we were sun-drenched, soaked, exhausted—and exhilarated. We hauled our boats to the trailer and climbed into the bus for the 40-minute ride back to town.When we got back to Ohiopyle, we saw a group of local kids jumping off a bridge into the river below. They told us it was safe—as long as you landed in the deep spot. Miss it, and you could get seriously hurt.That was enough for David. He wisely passed.
But Peter was intrigued.
I wasn’t sure it was safe for him, so I decided to try it first. I climbed up on the narrow guardrail.  The edge was uncomfortably narrow, and the drop was high enough to make you think twice.  I experienced a few butterflies of my own.
I jumped off, and it wasn’t so bad once you got past the initial fear. I decided it was safe enough for the boy—if he still wanted to try.
He said he did.
Peter climbed up onto the guardrail... and froze. He stood there. And stood there. And stood there.
Trying to help, I started a countdown:
“Three... two—”
“DON’T COUNT!” he shouted.
Then his face crumpled, and he began to cry—terrified.
I gently pulled him down and hugged him.
“You don’t have to do this,” I said. “Look how big David is, and he doesn't want to do it.”
Peter looked up at me, eyes still wet, and said,
“Dad, you have so many stories to tell because you do things like this. If I don’t do it... I won’t have any stories.”
I paused. He had a point.
So I helped him back up onto the rail.
“Okay,” I said. “Go ahead and jump.”
He was still scared, still teary—but he stood tall, took a breath, and whispered to himself:
“I’m gonna do it. I’m gonna do it.
And then—he jumped.
As he soared, he shouted:
“I’m gonna do iiiiiit!”
He popped up from the water with a huge grin—pure joy and pride lighting up his face.
Then he did it again.
And again.
And again.
When we got home, Peter retold the story—every jump, every rapid, every rescue—with the kind of energy only a nine-year-old can bring.
I just sat back, smiled, and listened.
He had his story now.  And it was a good one.
