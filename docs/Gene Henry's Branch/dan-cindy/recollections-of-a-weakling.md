Conflict Resolution as the Youngest of Four Brothers\
By Dan  Remembered in 2025

Story One: The F-BombThis is the story of the time I called my brother an "effer." You know what I mean by that, right?
Patrick and I used to get home from school by bus before Mom returned from work. Our older siblings were usually off doing their own thing, so it was just the two of us. Patrick knew exactly how to push my buttons. He was older, bigger, and stronger—and he loved to torture me.
One day, after relentless teasing, I finally snapped.
“Pat, you… FUCKER!”
Keep in mind, I grew up in a home where swearing was practically nonexistent. I had never heard my parents—or even my siblings—use profanity. My foul language vocabulary was limited, and the f-word was my nuclear option. It was my desperate attempt to reclaim some tiny shred of power.
Patrick froze, staring at me in disbelief. Then a slow, wicked grin spread across his face.
“I can’t wait for Mom to get home,” he said. “I’m going to tell her what you said—and you are so busted.”
I was doomed. I had never been in trouble for swearing before, and I imagined the consequences would be catastrophic.
Mom didn’t get home for another hour, and Patrick spent every minute gleefully reminding me of my impending doom. By the time she walked in the door, I was a nervous wreck.
Patrick sprinted to her.
“Mom! I have to tell you something! Before you got home, Dan said a really bad word. He called me… I can’t even say it. You know—the bad one. He called me an effer!”
Mom blinked. “What? What did your brother say?”
“You know! He called me an effer!”
Slowly, realization dawned on her face. She looked over at me, still cringing in the corner, and then turned to Patrick. Without a word, she grabbed him by the arm, spun him around, and gave him a swat for every syllable:
“What—did—you—do—to your brother—to make—him—say—such—a—bad—word?”
Then she walked over to me. But instead of punishment, she knelt in front of me and said gently:
“People who use bad words like that usually aren’t smart enough to say something better.”
I just nodded. “Okay, Mom.”
That was the first time I remember seriously wondering if there might be a higher power—someone who might actually be watching out for me.

Story Two: The Three-for-One RuleOf all my brothers, the one I clashed with the least was Jim.
Jim was the fun brother. He invented games—lake games, yard games, and elaborate night games that brought the whole neighborhood together. When word got out that Jim and Chip were organizing something, kids came from blocks away. My childhood often felt like a dream because of that.
But every once in a while, even Jim and I would have our conflicts.
We didn’t throw punches to the face or gut. Our fights were limited to arms and legs. Still, I usually came out on the bac end of things. I always had this unshakable faith that, no matter how mad I got, Jim wouldn’t actually hurt me too badly.
That said, sometimes I’d get so angry I’d haul back and take a swing at his arm or leg. Every time, Jim would calmly remind me of hia policy he called the “Three-for-One Rule.”
“Dan,” he’d say kindly, “I know you’re upset, and I know you want to hit me. But just remember: for every one hit you give me, I give three back. And not just in number—but in intensity. So think carefully.”
Usually, I’d end up giving him a light tap.
He’d congratulate me by saying. “Smart move. Now hold on—here come your three. They’ll be a little harder than yours, but hey, you made a wise choice keeping it light.”
And he’d give me three firm—but fair—pops on the arm.
Eventually, Patrick and Chip adopted Jim’s Three-for-One Rule too.
For the most part, I was more of a wrestler. I’d try to grab and throw them down, but I usually ended up pinned to the floor until I calmed down.
“Are you calm? Are you calm?” they’d ask.
Once they let me up, I’d march straight to their rooms, yank open their dresser drawers, and dump all their clothes on the floor. Sometimes I’d mess up their beds for good measure.
My room was always such a disaster that they couldn’t really retaliate.
“I win!”.

Story Three: Dad’s TowelIn a house with two working parents and six kids, there was always a lot of love—but rarely a clean towel.
Everyone knew the rule: Dad’s towel, which hung in his bathroom, was off limits. Touching it was a serious offense. Dad’s wrath wasn’t something anyone wanted to deal with.
But there were only two showers in the house: one in the middle bathroom, and one in Mom and Dad’s bathroom. The towels in the middle bath were usually damp and smelled vaguely of mildew. Dad’s towel? Dry. Clean. Perfect.
And irresistible.
Every once in a while, Dad would call all of us into the living room and ask, in that dreaded tone:
“Who used my towel?”
We’d all stand there, tight-lipped, while he repeated the question. One time, after a long silence, Jim finally said, “Dad, it was me.”
Dad stared at him.
“You’re just trying to protect your brothers. I know it wasn’t you. You can get out of here.”
Then he turned back to the rest of us.
“Now—who was it?”
I’m pretty sure it was either Patrick or me. It was almost always Patrick or me when something went wrong or missing.
Thanks anyway, Jim.

Making Stories in Ohiopyle, PennsylvaniaBy Dan
Peter was 9, and David was 12. We were with a large family group preparing to raft the Youghiogheny River—known to locals as “the Yawk”—in Ohiopyle State Park, about 65 miles southeast of Pittsburgh.
David had his own inflatable kayak—a “ducky”—and quickly proved himself a confident, capable paddler. Peter, on the other hand, was disappointed to learn he couldn’t paddle solo. Instead, he’d have to be in a raft or tandem boat. So, he and I teamed up in a double ducky.
When rafting the Youghiogheny, you’ve got two main options: the Upper River, mostly flat with gentle Class I and II rapids, or the Lower River, which features bigger, faster Class III and IV rapids—thrilling, but potentially dangerous, especially at high water. Our group was tackling the Lower.
With the park rangers’ green light, we pushed off. David paddled on his own, always in sight, clearly in his element.
At each major rapid, guides stationed on the banks shouted instructions to help us navigate. At first, Peter wasn’t thrilled to be stuck with me—but that changed once he discovered his purpose on the river.
After each rapid, we’d often see empty boats drifting solo—evidence that someone had dumped. That’s when Peter and I kicked into action.
We’d grab the paddle, then pull alongside the loose kayak. Peter would leap in, steer it back to its paddler—usually waiting on the shore—and hop back into our boat. We felt like river lifeguards. Eventually, we started hanging back on purpose, going last through the rapids to spot stragglers and lend a hand when needed.
Once, Kim—slightly older than Peter—was tossed from her raft and swept into a particularly large rapid. At first, she looked terrified. But then her expression changed—she lit up, grinning and waving like she was on a roller coaster. She crested a wave and landed gently on the edge of a large rock mid-rapid. She crawled to the high point of the rock.
There she was, stranded on a boulder in the middle of one of the biggest rapids—still smiling and waving as other boaters floated by.

Peter and I knew we had to time it just right. As we moved into position, we called for Kim to jump—and she did, landing in our boat mid-rapid. We ferried her back to her raft through the swirling water, all three of us laughing the whole way.By the end of the day, we were sun-drenched, soaked, exhausted—and exhilarated. We hauled our boats to the trailer and climbed into the bus for the 40-minute ride back to town.
When we got back to Ohiopyle, we saw a group of local kids jumping off a bridge into the river below. They told us it was safe—as long as you landed in the deep spot. Miss it, and you could get seriously hurt.
That was enough for David. He wisely passed.
But Peter was intrigued.
I wasn’t sure it was safe for him, so I decided to try it first. I climbed up on the narrow guardrail.  The edge was uncomfortably narrow, and the drop was high enough to make you think twice.  I experienced a few butterflies of my own.
I jumped off, and it wasn’t so bad once you got past the initial fear. I decided it was safe enough for the boy—if he still wanted to try.
He said he did.
Peter climbed up onto the guardrail... and froze. He stood there. And stood there. And stood there.
Trying to help, I started a countdown:
“Three... two—”
“DON’T COUNT!” he shouted.
Then his face crumpled, and he began to cry—terrified.
I gently pulled him down and hugged him.
“You don’t have to do this,” I said. “Look how big David is, and he doesn't want to do it.”
Peter looked up at me, eyes still wet, and said,
“Dad, you have so many stories to tell because you do things like this. If I don’t do it... I won’t have any stories.”
I paused. He had a point.
So I helped him back up onto the rail.
“Okay,” I said. “Go ahead and jump.”
He was still scared, still teary—but he stood tall, took a breath, and whispered to himself:
“I’m gonna do it. I’m gonna do it.
And then—he jumped.
As he soared, he shouted:
“I’m gonna do iiiiiit!”
He popped up from the water with a huge grin—pure joy and pride lighting up his face.
Then he did it again.
And again.
And again.
When we got home, Peter retold the story—every jump, every rapid, every rescue—with the kind of energy only a nine-year-old can bring.
I just sat back, smiled, and listened.
He had his story now.  And it was a good one.
