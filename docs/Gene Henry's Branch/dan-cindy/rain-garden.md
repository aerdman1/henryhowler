Cindy and I have a garden in the back that started with very poor soil. Over time, as we added organic matter and better soil, the garden bed became raised above the surrounding hillside. This caused a problem: during heavy rains, water would pool in the garden and swamp the vegetables.In 2023, I dug a deep trench through the garden and buried a 12-inch culvert to let water pass underneath. On the downhill side, the water flowed into a narrow ditch and continued on its way.
In August of 2025, Cindy and I attended a rain garden workshop with Linda and Casey. A rain garden is designed to capture runoff in a low spot, creating a wetland-like area where moisture-loving plants can thrive. Inspired by the workshop, I decided to build one here. This article is the start of that project—I’ll update it over time as the rain garden matures.
Step 1: Digging the Basin
I began by digging out an area on the downhill side of the culvert. Using the front-end loader on my small tractor, I scraped away a significant amount of clay soil to form a depression a few inches lower than the culvert outlet.

Step 2: Lining with StoneNext, I lined the basin with stone. I gathered rocks from a neighbor’s driveway project, where I helped remove about 300 large stones. Some went to Pat’s house for his project, others to Todd’s pond-building efforts, and the rest I stockpiled for myself. One large rectangular sandstone slab became the overflow outlet: when the rain garden fills, excess water will spill over this stone and onto the surrounding grass.Step 3: Installing the Pipe
To manage water flow, I placed a smaller pipe inside the culvert with the end turned upward. This way, water can still flow through the culvert without completely bypassing the rain garden. The design lets the depression gradually saturate with water while preventing flooding. I set the pipe’s outlet just below the level of the sandstone overflow, ensuring that excess water escapes once the basin fills.

Step 4: Adding SoilWith the boulders and pipe in place, I backfilled the depression with compost and topsoil. This created a planting area ready for moisture-loving plants that will make the rain garden thrive.

Planting the Rain GardenChoosing the right plants is key to a successful rain garden. They need to tolerate both wet conditions after a storm and dry periods between rains. I plan to use a mix of native flowers, grasses, and shrubs for resilience and habitat value.Suggested Plants for a Rain Garden
🌿 Grasses & Sedges (structure and root depth)
Switchgrass (Panicum virgatum)

Fox Sedge (Carex vulpinoidea)

Little Bluestem (Schizachyrium scoparium)

🌸 Perennials (color and pollinator value)Blue Flag Iris (Iris versicolor)

Purple Coneflower (Echinacea purpurea)

Black-eyed Susan (Rudbeckia hirta)

Swamp Milkweed (Asclepias incarnata) – great for monarch butterflies

🌳 Shrubs (height and wildlife cover)Red Twig Dogwood (Cornus sericea)

Buttonbush (Cephalanthus occidentalis)

Winterberry (Ilex verticillata)

I’ll start small, planting a few species and watching how they respond to the conditions. Over time, the rain garden should develop into a self-sustaining wetland habitat that manages runoff and supports pollinators, birds, and other wildlife.
